\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\section{Non-interactive segmentation performance}
\label{rq1results}
After running the non-interactive evaluation of the models' segmentation quality on the test set of 27 raw tablet images, it was clear that \acs{SAM} did not perform better than the classical baselines (see \cref{subsec:classical_comparison}).
It was also found that the tested \acs{ICL} variants of \acs{SAM} were beaten (see \cref{subsec:comparison_icl_sam}) by the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation, which through ablation was determined to be the original \acs{SAM} with classical threshold-derived point prompts, without any filter (see \cref{subsec:ablation_sam}).

The following subsections go in-depth with each of these three subresults, and what they mean for the research question at hand:


\subsection{Ablation of zero-shot \acs{SAM}}
\label{subsec:ablation_sam}

The results of the ablation study of the in-total 12 different permutations of \acs{SAM} (see \cref{fig:dice_sam_ablation}, and \cref{tab:sam-variants-summary}) showed that the best performing variant was the original \acs{SAM} with image encoder Vit-H, without any filter, guided by classical threshold-derived automatic point prompts.
This was closely followed by its bilateral pre-filtered variant, and its Mobile\acs{SAM} variant (which performed worse due to its smaller image encoder Tiny\acs{ViT}).

Surprisingly so, the \acs{SAM}2 variants performed worse than the original \acs{SAM}, both when and when not prompted.
The \acs{POI}-based prompting strategy demonstratively did not work on \acs{SAM}2, as shown by the \acs{SAM}2 variants with this strategy performing worse than when unprompted.
However, for the other vanilla (non-Hiera) \acs{ViT}-based \acs{SAM} variants, the \acs{POI}-based prompting strategy did work, and was even necessary to obtain any meaningful performance whatsoever.
This is not surprising, as zero-shot \acs{SAM} has never seen this type of images before, since it was exclusively trained on natural images.

\subsection{Comparison of \acs{ICL} \acs{SAM}}
\label{subsec:comparison_icl_sam}
Neither the one-shot \acs{GFSAM} nor the few-shot \acs{FATESAM} performed better (see \cref{fig:dice_icl_comparison}) than the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation (found in the previous \cref{subsec:ablation_sam}). 
\acs{GFSAM} performed particularly poorly with a mean \acs{Dice} score of approximately \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.14735636150568918}, obtaining roughly the same poor scores on hard images (\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.14699629000917605}), as with both medium (\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.14264739200706456}) and easy ones (\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.15242540250082692}). 
\acs{FATESAM}2D, however, performed relatively much better with a mean \acs{Dice} score of \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.25571675578635145}, which was comparable to the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation (with \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.26263640013862244}).
It seems that the \acs{ICL} strategy of \acs{GFSAM} is not effective within the domain in question, while the \acs{ICL} strategy of \acs{FATESAM}2D clearly works, but is still not enough to outperform zero-shot \acs{SAM}, when the latter has the right automatic prompting strategy.

\subsubsection{GFSAM}
\acs{GFSAM}'s particularly poor performance was clearly due to the fact that it - for almost all instances - segmented the whole tablet instead of the inscriptions within it (as shown in \cref{fig:comparison_icl_sam}).
This indicates that the one-shot strategy of \acs{GFSAM} is generally not effective for this domain of non-natural images. 
It is very likely that no point ever passed its gating processes, always returning the mask with the highest predicted \acs{IoU} score instead, which was the whole tablet, as it is always the most salient and clearly separated object in the image.
Specifically, \acs{GFSAM}'s overshooting gating process is likely to have been the culprit, as it is designed to filter out points that are near the boundary of a mask, which for a thin engraving is always the case.

\subsubsection{FATESAM2D}
\acs{FATESAM}2D's comparatively better performance can be attributed to the support images actually providing useful information to the model.
The variant with blank support images (\acs{FATESAM}2D-blank) performing worst of all (see \cref{fig:dice_icl_comparison}), is a proof of this, as the inference hence does not work without any meaningful support images.
The manipulation of \acs{SAM}2's memory encoder and memory attention mechanism thus worked reasonably well for few-shot inference given just the raw tablet images.

But still, this additional few-shot context was clearly not enough to outperform the threshold-derived \acs{POI}-based zero-shot \acs{SAM}.
As a general error, \acs{FATESAM}2D suffered from undersegmentation rather than oversegmentation (as seen in \cref{fig:comparison_icl_sam}).
This might have been due to the big class imbalance between foreground and background in the support images, with background dominating compared to the thin and small engravings.

Furthermore, the \acs{POI}-based prompting strategy did not work on \acs{FATESAM}2D, as shown in \cref{fig:dice_icl_comparison} by the variant using this strategy performing worse than when unprompted.
This fits with the observation from the previous \cref{subsec:ablation_sam} that this prompting strategy does not work on \acs{SAM}2, which is where \acs{FATESAM}2D gets its architecture from.

\subsection{Statistical significance}
\label{subsec:statistical_significance}
As it was clear from the results, the best tested variant of \acs{SAM} did not perform better than the best performing classical baseline.
To determine whether it performed \textit{significantly worse}, a paired t-test was conducted, as the paired differences in \acs{Dice} scores between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}-no-filter were approximately normally distributed (see next \cref{subsubsec:normality_residuals}).
What follows is the documentation of that normality test, the paired t-test, and the results of both.

\subsubsection{Normality of paired differences}
\label{subsubsec:normality_residuals}
As the QQplot in \cref{fig:qqplot} shows, the paired differences in \acs{Dice} scores between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}-no-filter appeared to be approximately normally distributed.
Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality of these paired differences yielded a p-value of $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.0562033322217413} > \alpha = 0.05$, indicating that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected.
Thus, there was no need for a parametric test like Wilcoxon, as the normality assumption for the paired t-test was satisfied, and its results could be considered valid.
\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../src/data/results/evaluation/qqplots/Dice_Gaussian_vs_SAM+pts-no-filter.png}
    \caption{QQplot of the paired differences between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}-no-filter. They appear to be approximately normally distributed, which supports the validity of the paired t-test results. 
    Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test on the normality of these paired differences yielded a p-value of $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.0562033322217413} > \alpha = 0.05$, indicating that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected.}
    \label{fig:qqplot}
\end{figure}

\clearpage

\subsubsection{Paired t-test}
A one-tailed paired t-test was conducted on the paired differences $d_i$ in \acs{Dice} scores obtained for each of the 27 test images across \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}-no-filter (with $d_i = Dice_{Gaussian,i} - Dice_{SAM+pts-no-filter,i}$) under the following hypotheses:


\begin{itemize}
\item $H_0$: There is no difference in mean \acs{Dice} score between the two models (or \acs{SAM} has a higher mean).$$\mu_{d} \leq 0$$
\item $H_1$: \acs{Gaussian} has a higher mean \acs{Dice} score than \acs{SAM+pts}-no-filter.$$\mu_{d} > 0$$
\end{itemize}

Performing the paired t-test yielded a test statistic of $t \approx \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{3.73744358804111}$ and a p-value of $P(T > t) \approx \pgfmathprintnumber[sci,precision=3]{0.0004618332770736502}$.
Thus, as $\pgfmathprintnumber[sci,precision=3]{0.0004618332770736502} < \alpha = 0.05$, the null hypothesis could be rejected, and it could be concluded that \acs{Gaussian} significantly outperformed \acs{SAM+pts}-no-filter in terms of \acs{Dice} score.
In other words, the best tested variant of \acs{SAM} clearly performed \textit{significantly worse} in comparison to the best tested classical segmentation method for non-interactive segmentation of Linear A tablets.

\section{Workflow impact}

The second case study of the 3 documents showed that none of the two tools were able to save time in the workflow, as the total workflow time spent was higher than the time spent on manual annotation in Krita (see \cref{fig:workflow_time}).
Even just based on the time spent on post-editing alone, the tools did not save time compared to just drawing manually from scratch.
In fact, the total time spent doing the classical baseline workflow was more than twice the time spent doing the manual one, while the time spent doing the \acs{SAM}-based workflow was more than thrice (and almost four times) the manual workflow time.

\section{Consolidated discussion}
The results suggest that \acs{SAM} in the forms tested is not suitable for segmentation of Linear A tablets.
For each \acs{RQ}, \acs{SAM} failed compared to the baselines - even when guided by human interaction.
Not only did it not live up to its supposedly \acs{SOTA} superiority in terms of mere segmentation quality.
It also took more time to use, and did not save any time in the workflow whatsoever.

Nevertheless, it is important to point out that the results of the two case studies (in contrast to the non-interactive evaluation) - due to the small sample size of 3 documents, and the fact that only one user (Dr. Ester Salgarella) was involved - are not statistically generalizable.
Thus, the results of these case studies should be interpreted as indicative of the \textit{potential} impact of the tools on the workflow, rather than conclusive evidence of their actual impact.
Still, none of the data obtained showed potential for time-saving.
And none showed potential for \acs{SAM} outperforming the best classical baseline.

When taking the domain and data quality into account, this underperformance is not too surprising.
During the second case study, Dr. Salgarella gave the impression that even she herself could not clearly distinguish tablet damage from inscriptions based on the raw images alone.
If even an expert cannot do that, it is not surprising that a model that has not seen this type of images before cannot do it either.

But what these results suggest, is not only that the available data is poor.
As \acs{SAM} performed worse than the bestclassical baseline on the same data, it also tells us that \acs{SAM}'s generalization capabilities are not sufficient enough within this domain.
For \acs{SAM} to be useful, not only is there a need for a more suitable data source, there is seemingly also a need for a sufficiently sized dataset to be able to adapt \acs{SAM} to the domain.
To allow for a hypothetical dataset of such size, it have to also be composed of inscriptions other than Linear A - at least including the other closely related Aegean scripts like Linear B, but maybe also epigraphical inscriptions in general - forming a generalized inscription segmentation dataset, which could then lay the groundwork for the creation of an epigraphically tuned \acs{SAM}. 

Until such a model is developed, however, the results suggest that classical deterministic methods are better suited for the task.
And most importantly, they suggest that manual annotation in Krita remains the best option for producing drawings of Linear A documents.
Nevertheless, that is not to say that there is no potential for automation in the workflow.
Rather, it means that the tested approaches are not sufficient enough to be helpful for it, and that alternative approaches should be explored in future work.
For instance, if ignoring \acs{IPR} issues and taking the already made expert drawings from the \acs{GORILA} corpus as input instead of the raw document images, it is in theory entirely doable to cut drawing time through little else than a binarization, rescaling and smoothing of those drawings, giving Ester the most qualified suggested starting point that there is.
Doing so, however, was not within the scope of this thesis, as it was focused on the process of creating drawings from scratch.
But in Ester's case specifically, it represents a promising workaround and pivot for future work, as it does not depend on transformers nor any segmentation models whatsoever.
