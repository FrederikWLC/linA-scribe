\section{Non-interactive segmentation performance}
\label{rq1results}
After running the non-interactive segmentation evaluation of the models on the dataset (hyperparameter optimization results can be found in \cref{appendix:hyperparam_optimization}), it was clear that \acs{mSAM} did not perform better than the classical baselines (see \cref{fig:dice_comparison} for a plot of the results).

Meanwhile, \acs{SAM+pts} with the engineered point prompt clouds performed considerably better.
With a mean \acs{Dice} score of around \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\SAMptsmean}, it performed better than \acs{Otsu} and \acs{CannyFill} (with mean \acs{Dice} scores of \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\Otsumean} and \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\CannyFillmean}, respectively), and comparably to the best performing classical baseline, \acs{Gaussian} that yielded a \acs{Dice} score of \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\Gaussianmean}.

The following subsections go into a bit more detail on the explanation of these results, the statistical significance of the differences in performance between \acs{SAM} and the best classical baseline (see \cref{appendix:non-interactive-evaluation-data} in the appendix for the full results table), and a discussion of the fairness of this experiment.
\subsection{SAM ablation}

The results of the ablation study of different SAM variants (see \cref{fig:dice_sam_ablation}) showed that the best performing variant was the one with automatic point prompts and bilateral filter, making use of the single output token per image instead of best of three.
This was also the one used for the main evaluation.

Knowing the overall quantitative results (see \cref{fig:dice_comparison}), it pays to look at a visual example to see what is going on behind the scenes.

\acs{CannyFill} also oversegments, due to its large kernel window size for morphological closing, which was necessary to fill in the gaps between edges, but gives a blocky look, and merges all nearby edges together.

The unprompted \acs{SAM} segments only background noise here, producing nothing of value.
But the prompted \acs{SAM+pts} does produce a somewhat coherent mask, capturing almost every sign with less oversegmented areas than both \acs{Otsu} and \acs{CannyFill}.
Still, it oversegments many signs (especially those with background-space supposed to be inside of them), fades out signs where they are supposed to continue (due to loss of attention, as segmenting many separated signs at once is not what its attention weights have been trained for), and is not able to capture the outline of the tablet.
But the signs that it does capture well, are captured with a good level of spatial coherence.
And there is way less noise between them compared to the masks of the baselines, which is arguably a positive sign for the potential of \acs{SAM} to perform well on this task once properly fine-tuned prompts, or points are placed through human supervision.

\subsection{Statistical significance}
As it was clear from the results, \acs{SAM+pts} did not perform better than the best performing classical baseline.
To determine whether it performed significantly worse, a paired t-test was conducted, as residuals of the differences in \acs{Dice} scores between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts} were approximately normally distributed (see next \cref{subsubsec:normality_residuals}).
What follows is the documentation of that normality test, the paired t-test, and the results of both.

\subsubsection{Normality of residuals}
\label{subsubsec:normality_residuals}
As the QQplot in \cref{fig:qqplot} shows, the residuals of the paired differences in \acs{Dice} scores between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts} appeared to be approximately normally distributed.
Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality of these residuals yielded a p-value of $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\ShapiroPValueResidualGaussianSAM} > \alpha = 0.05$, indicating that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected.
Thus, the normality assumption for the paired t-test was satisfied, and its results could be considered valid.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../src/data/results/evaluation/qqplots/Dice_Gaussian_vs_SAM+pts-no-filter.png}
    \caption{QQplot of the residuals of the paired t-test comparing \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}. The residuals appear to be approximately normally distributed, which supports the validity of the paired t-test results. 
    Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality of these residuals yielded a p-value of $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\ShapiroPValueResidualGaussianSAM} > \alpha = 0.05$, indicating that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected.}
    \label{fig:qqplot}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Paired t-test}
The paired t-test was conducted on the \acs{Dice} scores obtained for each of the $\pgfmathprintnumber{\numberofimages}$ images across \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}.
The null hypothesis ($H_0$) stated that there is no difference in mean \acs{Dice} scores between the two models, while the alternative hypothesis ($H_1$) stated that \acs{Gaussian} has a higher mean \acs{Dice} score than \acs{SAM+pts}.

Performing the paired t-test yielded a test statistic of $t \approx \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\tteststatGaussianSAM}$ and a p-value of $P(T > t) \approx \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\ttestpvalueGaussianSAM}$.
Thus, as $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\ttestpvalueGaussianSAM} < \alpha = 0.05$, the null hypothesis could be rejected, and it could be concluded that \acs{Gaussian} significantly outperforms \acs{SAM+pts} in terms of \acs{Dice} score.
Hence, it can be said that \acs{SAM} performed significantly worse in comparison to the best classical segmentation methods for non-interactive segmentation of Linear A tablets, even when automatically prompted.

\subsection{Fairness of the experiment}

\subsubsection{Data}
The dataset used for evaluation was the same as the one used for hyperparameter optimization of models, which could be seen as a potential source of bias.
Additionally, the dataset of 60 images is quite small, which makes it more susceptible to noise and outliers, although sufficiently sized to support the statistical analysis.
Moreover, the ground truth annotations of the dataset were heavily biased, even as they were manually created and registered to the raw images.
Therefore, \acs{Dice} scores obtained from the evaluation were unreasonably low, and the performance of the models is not properly reflected by these scores.
However, for comparison between models, the relative differences in \acs{Dice} scores were still meaningful, as all models were evaluated on the same imperfectly aligned dataset.

\subsubsection{Model implementation}
In the case of the fairness of the \acs{SAM} implementation chosen, the experiment obviously only evaluates the performance of an untuned version.
This implementation had most certainly never seen Linear A tablet images during training, as it was pretrained by default on the large and diverse dataset of natural images \acs{SA-1B}.
Furthermore, the model implementation used makes use of Mobile\acs{SAM} with its small \acs{ViT} image encoder TinyVit \parencite{TinyViT}, which can compromise on segmentation quality.
Lastly, \acs{SAM} was restricted by the automatic nature of this experiment, as its intended use is interactive segmentation.
Thus, it does not come as a surprise that \acs{mSAM} underperforms in comparison to the classical baselines.
The results therefore suggest a need for human intervention for the optimal placement of prompts to provide the best conditions for \acs{SAM}, as it otherwise underperforms.

\section{Tool-side segmentation performance}
The case study of 3 documents showed that the interactive version of \acs{SAM}2 with box prompts and point prompts not only took more time to use than the classical baseline, due to the burden of interaction, but also did not perform better in terms of segmentation quality, even with the help of human input (see \cref{fig:dice_by_tool_time}).

 A significant portion of the time spent using the interactive version of \acs{SAM}2 was on waiting for the model to execute through Modal (see \cref{fig:model_exec_time}).
 But clearly this was not the bottleneck, as the time spent on interaction was much higher, taking up more than 80\% of tool time.
 The time spent waiting on \acs{SAM} execution was also below 5 seconds more than 50\% of executions.

\section{Workflow impact}

The case study of 3 documents showed that none of the two tools were able to save time in the workflow, as the total workflow time spent was higher than the time spent on manual annotation in Krita (see \cref{fig:workflow_time}).
Even just based on the time spent on post-editing alone, the tools did not save time compared to just drawing manually from scratch.
In fact, the total time spent doing the classical workflow was more than twice the time spent doing the manual one, while the time spent doing the \acs{SAM}-based workflow was more than thrice (and almost four times) the time spent on the manual one.
