\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\section{Non-interactive segmentation performance}
\label{rq1results}
After running the non-interactive evaluation of the models' segmentation quality on the test set of 27 raw tablet images (hyperparameter optimization results can be found in \cref{appendix:hyperparam_optimization}), it was clear that \acs{SAM} did not perform better than the classical baselines (see \cref{subsec:classical_comparison}).
It was also found that the tested \acs{ICL} variants of \acs{SAM} were beaten (see \cref{subsec:comparison_icl_sam}) by the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation, which through ablation was determined to be the original \acs{SAM} with classical threshold-derived point prompts, without any filter (see \cref{subsec:ablation_sam}).

The following subsections go in-depth with each of these three subresults, and what they mean for the research question at hand:

\subsection{Ablation of zero-shot \acs{SAM}}
\label{subsec:ablation_sam}

The results of the ablation study of the in-total 12 different permutations of \acs{SAM} (see \cref{fig:dice_sam_ablation}, and \cref{tab:sam-variants-summary}) showed that the best performing variant was the original \acs{SAM} with image encoder Vit-H, without any filter, guided by classical threshold-derived automatic point prompts.
This was closely followed by its bilateral pre-filtered variant, and its Mobile\acs{SAM} variant (which performed worse due to its smaller image encoder Tiny\acs{ViT}).

Surprisingly so, the \acs{SAM}2 variants performed worse than the original \acs{SAM}, both when and when not prompted.
The \acs{POI}-based prompting strategy demonstratively did not work on \acs{SAM}2, as shown by the \acs{SAM}2 variants with this strategy performing worse than when unprompted.
However, for the other vanilla (non-Hiera) \acs{ViT}-based \acs{SAM} variants, the \acs{POI}-based prompting strategy did work, and was even necessary to obtain any meaningful performance whatsoever.
This is not surprising, as zero-shot \acs{SAM} has never seen this type of images before, since it was exclusively trained on natural images.
Thus, it can be stated that when it comes to images of inscribed tablets of Linear A, the model does not know what to segment, without any external guidance.

\subsection{Comparison of \acs{ICL} \acs{SAM}}
\label{subsec:comparison_icl_sam}
Neither the one-shot \acs{GFSAM} nor the few-shot \acs{FATESAM} performed better (see \cref{fig:dice_icl_comparison}) than the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation (found in the previous \cref{subsec:ablation_sam}). 
\acs{GFSAM} performed particularly poorly with a mean \acs{Dice} score of approximately \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.14735636150568918}, obtaining roughly the same poor scores on hard images (\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.14699629000917605}), as with both medium (\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.14264739200706456}) and easy ones (\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.15242540250082692}). 
\acs{FATESAM}2D, however, performed relatively way better with a mean \acs{Dice} score of \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.25571675578635145}, which was comparable to the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation (with \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.26263640013862244}), although still worse.

This fits with the observation from the previous \cref{subsec:ablation_sam} that this prompting strategy does not work on \acs{SAM}2, which is where \acs{FATESAM}2D  gets its architecture from.

Furthermore, the \acs{FATESAM}2D variant with blank support images (\acs{FATESAM}2DBlank) performed worst of all.
This supports the idea that the support images provided useful information to the main model, even though it was not able to effectively leverage this information to perform better than the best zero-shot \acs{SAM} implementation.
Hence, it can be concluded that the \acs{ICL} strategy of \acs{GFSAM} is not effective within the domain in question, while the \acs{ICL} strategy of \acs{FATESAM}2D clearly works, but is still not enough to outperform zero-shot \acs{SAM} with the right automatic prompting strategy.

\subsection{Statistical significance}
\label{subsec:statistical_significance}
As it was clear from the results, \acs{SAM+pts} did not perform better than the best performing classical baseline.
To determine whether it performed significantly worse, a paired t-test was conducted, as residuals of the differences in \acs{Dice} scores between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts} were approximately normally distributed (see next \cref{subsubsec:normality_residuals}).
What follows is the documentation of that normality test, the paired t-test, and the results of both.

\subsubsection{Normality of residuals}
\label{subsubsec:normality_residuals}
As the QQplot in \cref{fig:qqplot} shows, the residuals of the paired differences in \acs{Dice} scores between \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts} appeared to be approximately normally distributed.
Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality of these residuals yielded a p-value of $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\ShapiroPValueResidualGaussianSAM} > \alpha = 0.05$, indicating that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected.
Thus, the normality assumption for the paired t-test was satisfied, and its results could be considered valid.
\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../src/data/results/evaluation/qqplots/Dice_Gaussian_vs_SAM+pts-no-filter.png}
    \caption{QQplot of the residuals of the paired t-test comparing \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM+pts}. The residuals appear to be approximately normally distributed, which supports the validity of the paired t-test results. 
    Furthermore, the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality of these residuals yielded a p-value of $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{\ShapiroPValueResidualGaussianSAM} > \alpha = 0.05$, indicating that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected.}
    \label{fig:qqplot}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Paired t-test}
The paired t-test was conducted on the \acs{Dice} scores obtained for each of the $\pgfmathprintnumber{\numberofimages}$ images across \acs{Gaussian} and \acs{SAM} under the following hypotheses:


\begin{itemize}
\item $H_0$: There is no difference in mean \acs{Dice} scores between the two models.$$\mu_{Gaussian} = \mu_{SAM}$$
\item $H_1$: \acs{Gaussian} has a higher mean \acs{Dice} score than \acs{SAM}.$$\mu_{Gaussian} > \mu_{SAM}$$
\end{itemize}

Performing the paired t-test yielded a test statistic of $t \approx \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{3.73744358804111}$ and a p-value of $P(T > t) \approx \pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.0009236665541473004}$.
Thus, as $\pgfmathprintnumber[fixed,precision=3]{0.0009236665541473004} < \alpha = 0.05$, the null hypothesis could be rejected, and it could be concluded that \acs{Gaussian} significantly outperformed \acs{SAM} in terms of \acs{Dice} score.
Hence, it can be said that the best testedvariant of \acs{SAM} performed significantly worse in comparison to the best classical segmentation methods for non-interactive segmentation of Linear A tablets.

\subsection{Fairness of the experiment}

\subsubsection{Model implementation}
In the case of the fairness of the \acs{SAM} implementation chosen, the experiment obviously only evaluates the performance of an untuned version.
This implementation had most certainly never seen Linear A tablet images during training, as it was pretrained by default on the large and diverse dataset of natural images \acs{SA-1B}.
Furthermore, \acs{SAM} was restricted by the automatic nature of this experiment, as its intended use is interactive segmentation.
Thus, it does not come as a surprise that \acs{SAM} underperforms in comparison to the classical baselines.
The results therefore suggest a need for human intervention for the optimal placement of prompts to provide the best conditions for \acs{SAM}, as it otherwise underperforms.

\section{Workflow impact}

The case study of 3 documents showed that none of the two tools were able to save time in the workflow, as the total workflow time spent was higher than the time spent on manual annotation in Krita (see \cref{fig:workflow_time}).
Even just based on the time spent on post-editing alone, the tools did not save time compared to just drawing manually from scratch.
In fact, the total time spent doing the classical workflow was more than twice the time spent doing the manual one, while the time spent doing the \acs{SAM}-based workflow was more than thrice (and almost four times) the time spent on the manual one.

\section{Consolidated discussion}
The results suggest that \acs{SAM} in the forms tested is not suitable for segmentation of Linear A tablets.
For each \acs{RQ}, \acs{SAM} failed compared to the baselines - even when guided by human interaction.
Not only did it not live up to its supposedly \acs{SOTA} superiority in terms of mere segmentation quality.
It also took more time to use, and did not save any time in the workflow whatsoever.

When taking the domain and data quality into account, this is not too surprising.
